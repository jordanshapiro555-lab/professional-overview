The 5 Most Common CRO Mistakes I See on Healthcare Sites

Jordan Shapiro February 10, 2026  ·  5 min read

Healthcare and insurance sites operate in one of the most high-stakes conversion environments on the web. Users arrive anxious, often confused, making decisions that directly affect their health and finances. The margin for friction is effectively zero.

After running 250+ experiments across health insurance, health technology, and Medicare platforms, I've seen the same conversion killers appear again and again. Here are the five mistakes I see most often — and why they're so damaging in a healthcare context specifically.

1. Not Developing Enough Trust

Healthcare decisions are high-anxiety decisions. Users aren't just shopping — they're evaluating whether they can trust you with their coverage, their family, and their money. Yet most healthcare sites treat trust signals as an afterthought: a small BBB badge in the footer, a single client logo strip, and a "We take your privacy seriously" line buried in the form.

That's not nearly enough. Trust needs to be woven into the entire experience — in the headline, in the social proof proximate to the CTA, in the testimonials that speak to specific fears (cost, coverage gaps, enrollment complexity), and in the visual design itself. Users are pattern-matching for legitimacy at every scroll depth.

In my work with UnitedHealthcare, we saw meaningful lift from experiments that did nothing more than surface more contextually relevant trust signals — star ratings, coverage statistics, and real customer outcomes — at the moments in the funnel where anxiety peaks.

2. Asking for Required Information Without Explaining Why

Healthcare forms ask for personal information that users are understandably reluctant to share: date of birth, zip code, household income, existing conditions. Most sites just surface these fields with no explanation — and watch abandonment spike at each sensitive question.

The fix is simple but consistently skipped: tell users why you need the information and what it unlocks for them. "We need your zip code to show you plans available in your area" is more persuasive than an unlabeled zip code field with a required asterisk. "Your date of birth helps us find age-appropriate plan options" is more persuasive than nothing.

Explaining the purpose of each data request doesn't just reduce anxiety — it reframes the form fill as something you're doing for the user rather than to them. That shift in framing reliably reduces abandonment at friction points.

3. Missing Continuous Reassurance During Form Fill

Most healthcare sites front-load their trust messaging — big headlines, hero images, badges above the fold — then let it disappear the moment users enter the form. This is exactly backwards from how anxiety works in a multi-step conversion flow.

Trust anxiety doesn't diminish as users progress through a form. It often increases, because commitment is escalating. By the time someone reaches the step where they're asked for their SSN, primary care provider, or payment details, the site has often gone completely silent on reassurance.

Continuous reassurance means trust signals that follow users through the funnel: progress indicators that reduce perceived effort, inline confirmation of privacy and security at sensitive fields, reminders of what they're getting in exchange for their information, and clear "you can always go back" affordances that reduce the perceived cost of proceeding.

4. Overly Complex Language

Healthcare is riddled with industry jargon — deductibles, coinsurance, formularies, EOBs, HMOs vs. PPOs vs. EPOs. Experts in the space forget how confusing this language is to the average person shopping for coverage for the first time.

The result is copy that reads like a policy document rather than a conversation. Users disengage not because they're uninterested, but because they hit three unfamiliar terms in a row and decide it's too much cognitive work to continue.

The standard I try to apply: if a reasonably intelligent person without insurance industry experience would need to look up the term, it needs to be explained or replaced. Tooltips help for unavoidable jargon. Plain-language rewrites of headline copy help everywhere else. In my competitive auditing work, I consistently find that the highest-converting health insurance sites write at a 6th–8th grade reading level — not because their users are unsophisticated, but because clear language is simply faster to process and less risky to act on.

5. Making Product Comparison Difficult

Health insurance is, fundamentally, a comparison decision. Nobody buys the first plan they see. They want to evaluate options side by side — premium vs. deductible, network breadth, prescription coverage, out-of-pocket maximums.

Yet many insurance and health plan sites make this comparison nearly impossible. Plans are presented in non-parallel formats. Key differentiating data points are buried or inconsistently displayed. Mobile layouts collapse comparison tables into stacked cards that require extensive scrolling to mentally reconstruct.

One of the highest-leverage experiments I've run is simply improving the information architecture of plan comparison — standardizing what data appears for each plan, making the trade-off between cost and coverage immediately legible, and surfacing a "recommended for you" option based on user inputs. When users can actually compare, they convert faster and with less second-guessing.

The Common Thread

All five of these mistakes share the same root cause: the site is optimized for the organization's communication goals rather than the user's decision-making process. Healthcare users aren't looking for information dumps — they're looking for the confidence to act. CRO on healthcare sites is fundamentally the work of building that confidence at every step in the funnel.
