How I Measure CRO Program ROI (With Real Numbers)

Jordan Shapiro March 15, 2026  ·  8 min read

One of the most common questions I get from prospective clients is some version of: "How do you actually prove that CRO is working?" It's a fair question. Most programs report win rate and call it a day. But win rate alone tells you almost nothing about business impact.

Over time, I've developed a methodology that connects experiment outcomes directly to revenue — accounting for correlation strength, attribution share, novelty effect, and behavioral decay over time. This post walks through the exact process I used to calculate a 1,061% ROI for UHOne's 2024 optimization program.

Why Standard Reporting Falls Short

The typical CRO report looks like this: "We ran 30 tests, 12 won, 18 lost or were inconclusive. Here are the winning treatments." That's fine as a test log, but it says nothing about the dollar impact on the business. It doesn't account for:

  • How many sessions were actually affected by your winning experiments
  • How strongly your primary KPI correlates with revenue
  • How much of that correlation is actually caused by the KPI vs. confounding variables
  • The novelty effect — the tendency for users to engage differently with new experiences that fades over time

My methodology addresses all four of these. Here's how it works.

Step 1: Export All Site Data for the Measurement Year

Start by pulling a full export of session-level and transaction-level data for the year you're measuring. You want the entire year — not just the weeks tests were live — because you'll be running correlations that require population-level data to be meaningful.

At minimum, you need: sessions by page, conversion events (form submits, purchases, quote starts), and revenue (or revenue proxy). If your analytics platform separates test traffic from organic traffic, keep them together for this step — you're looking for population-level relationships.

Step 2: Calculate Sessions Impacted by Each Experiment

For each statistically significant experiment, calculate the total sessions that were actually influenced by the winning variation:

  • During testing: sessions exposed to the variation while the test was live
  • Post-test targeting: sessions on that page or segment after you applied the winner as a permanent change or targeted rule

Add these together. This is your "impacted sessions" denominator for each test — the universe of users whose experience was meaningfully changed by your program.

Step 3: Run Correlations Between Your Primary KPI and Revenue

For each experiment, you had a primary KPI — something like cart addition rate, form start rate, or click-through rate to a key page. Now you need to understand how much that KPI actually moves revenue.

Pull your annual dataset and run a Pearson correlation between the primary KPI (at the appropriate unit of analysis — visit, session, or user) and revenue. Plot it. You'll often find strong relationships between upstream engagement KPIs and downstream revenue that aren't visible when you look at tests in isolation.

The scatter plot above shows one of the correlations from the UHOne program. Each data point is a weekly cohort. The linear relationship is strong — meaning changes in cart addition rate reliably predict changes in revenue at the program level.

Step 4: Run a Linear Regression on the KPI and Revenue

Correlation tells you whether two variables move together. Regression tells you the magnitude of that relationship — and its explanatory power. For each KPI-revenue pair, run an OLS linear regression and record:

  • The slope (coefficient): how many dollars of revenue correspond to a one-unit increase in the KPI
  • R²: the proportion of variance in revenue explained by the KPI

The regression equation from the chart above — y = 2265.3x + 2.9916 — means that for every 1 percentage point increase in cart additions per visit, you'd expect roughly $22.65 more in cart LTV revenue per visit across the population.

Step 5: Multiply Correlation Coefficient × R² to Get Attributable Impact

Here's where most methodologies stop — and where mine gets more conservative and defensible. The correlation coefficient tells you how strongly the KPI and revenue move together. But the R² tells you how much of the revenue variance is actually explained by your KPI, as opposed to other factors (seasonality, external campaigns, pricing changes, etc.).

To isolate the portion of the correlation that's directly attributable to changes in your KPI:

Attribution Factor = Correlation Coefficient × R²

This gives you a conservative, defensible estimate of how much of the observed revenue movement is caused by changes in your tested KPI.

Step 6: Calculate Expected Revenue Change Per Experiment

Now you have everything you need for each test:

  1. Impacted sessions (from Step 2)
  2. The delta in your primary KPI observed during testing (variation vs. control)
  3. The regression coefficient (from Step 4)
  4. The attribution factor (correlation coefficient × R², from Step 5)

Plug them together:

Expected Revenue Change = Impacted Sessions × KPI Delta × Regression Coefficient × Attribution Factor

This gives you the expected revenue impact for each winning experiment, before accounting for behavioral decay.

Step 7: Apply the Depreciation Curve

One of the most overlooked problems in CRO reporting is the novelty effect. When users first encounter a new design or message, they often engage with it more than they will over the long term — simply because it's new. If you use the KPI delta from a 2-week test window to project full-year revenue, you'll significantly overstate impact.

To correct for this, I apply a depreciation curve that discounts the expected KPI lift based on how long ago the test ended:

The depreciation schedule I use:

  • Phase 1 (0–90 days post-test): 10% depreciation
  • Phase 2 (91–180 days): 30% depreciation
  • Phase 3 (181–270 days): 40% depreciation
  • Phase 4 (271–360 days): 60% depreciation
  • Phase 5 (361+ days): 80% depreciation

For each period, the formula becomes:

Depreciated Revenue = Expected Revenue Change × (1 − Depreciation Rate)

Applied segment by segment as post-test time accrues.

Summing the depreciated revenue estimates across all time phases and all winning experiments gives you a conservative, well-reasoned estimate of total program revenue impact.

What This Looks Like in Practice

For UHOne's 2024 program, applying this methodology across all significant experiments produced a total estimated revenue impact of approximately $650K in incremental revenue against a program investment of roughly $60K — resulting in a documented 1,061% ROI.

That number is defensible because it's built on:

  • Population-level correlation data, not test-window-only estimates
  • Conservative attribution that doesn't claim 100% credit for revenue correlated with a KPI
  • A depreciation curve that accounts for novelty effect decay

It's not a perfect model — no attribution model is. But it's far more rigorous than the alternative, and it gives leadership a credible, replicable framework to evaluate program investment year over year.

A Note on Tooling

I run all of this in spreadsheets — no proprietary software required. The correlation and regression calculations are standard Excel or Google Sheets functions. The depreciation schedule is a lookup table. What makes this methodology valuable isn't the tooling; it's the intellectual framework for thinking about how test outcomes translate to business outcomes over time.

If you're running a CRO program and not measuring it this way, you're almost certainly either overstating or understating your impact. Neither is good for making the case for continued investment.
